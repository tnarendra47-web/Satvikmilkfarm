How to Make Khoya (Mawa) at Home from Milk: Step by Step

By Satvik Milk Farm, Kumbhariya, Borlai (Pardi taluka, Valsad) · Updated 19 November 2026

Quick answer: Pour 1 litre of whole milk (buffalo or full-fat) into a heavy-bottomed kadai, bring it to a boil, then keep it on a low flame for about 2 hours, stirring every few minutes and scraping the dried milk from the sides back in. When the milk thickens like rabdi, then turns grainy and finally comes together as one soft mass that leaves the pan, switch off. Expect roughly 180–250 g of khoya per litre; buffalo milk gives more than cow milk. Cool, cover and refrigerate. Khoya, also called mawa, khoa or kova, is milk that has been slowly cooked in an open pan until most of its water has evaporated. It is the base for peda, burfi, gulab jamun, kalakand and many Diwali and wedding sweets in Gujarat and across India. What is khoya (mawa)?

Dairy-technology material describes khoya as milk concentrated about five times by open-pan heating, to roughly 56–70% total solids. Indian standards quoted in the same source set a maximum of 28% moisture and a minimum of 20% fat for khoa. Because the milk sugar is concentrated too, khoya tastes naturally sweet and nutty even though no sugar is added.

Three types: pindi, danedar and dhap

TypeTextureTypical use
PindiFirm, smooth, rolled into a ball or pat; driestBurfi, peda
DanedarGrainy (made from slightly acidic milk)Kalakand, milk cake
DhapSoft, sticky; most moistureGulab jamun, pantua

At home you choose the type mainly by when you stop cooking: stop earlier for soft khoya for gulab jamun, and cook a little longer for firm khoya for peda or burfi.

Which milk makes the best khoya?

  • Buffalo milk is preferred in dairy-technology texts: its higher fat and solids give more khoya and a whiter, softer, smoother texture that suits sweets.
  • Cow milk works too, but its khoya is usually harder and drier, and the yield is lower.
  • Use whole milk. The same sources say low-fat milk gives hard, coarse khoya, so skimmed milk is not a good choice.
  • Use fresh milk. Milk that has started to turn sour makes grainy, sour-tasting khoya.

For more on the difference, read cow milk vs buffalo milk.

Step-by-step: how to make khoya at home from milk

What you need

  • 1–2 litres of fresh whole milk (buffalo or full-fat cow milk)
  • A wide, heavy-bottomed kadai or non-stick pan, deep enough that the milk does not boil over
  • A flat wooden or silicone spatula that reaches the base and the sides
  • A clean, dry container with a lid

Method

  1. Boil: pour the milk into the pan and bring it to a gentle boil on a low to medium flame.
  2. Simmer low: turn the flame to low. Stir every few minutes, making sure the spatula touches the base so nothing sticks.
  3. Scrape the sides: milk solids dry on the sides of the pan. Scrape them back into the milk each time you stir.
  4. Watch it thicken: after about 45 minutes the milk will have reduced to about half. Later it looks like thick rabdi, then turns grainy.
  5. Stir constantly at the end: in the last 10–15 minutes, bubbles burst through the thick mass. Stir without stopping now; this is when it can burn.
  6. Stop at the right time: switch off when the mass comes together, stops bubbling and starts to leave the sides of the pan. It will firm up more as it cools.
  7. Cool and store: scrape everything into a bowl, cover and let it cool to room temperature, then refrigerate.

Home recipes report the whole process takes about 1.5 to 2.5 hours for 1 litre on a low flame. A wider pan evaporates water faster.

How much khoya will 1 litre of milk give?

Published home recipes report about 180–250 g of khoya from 1 litre of whole milk. Dairy-technology course material gives typical yields of 21.6–23% from buffalo milk and 18.3–18.5% from cow milk, or roughly 1 kg of khoya from 4 kg of buffalo milk or 5 kg of cow milk. Softer khoya (more moisture) weighs more; firm khoya weighs less. Watered-down milk gives less khoya and takes longer.

How to store homemade khoya

  • Keep it in a covered container in the fridge at 4 °C or below. Recipe guides suggest using it within about 3–7 days.
  • For longer storage, freeze it in small portions and thaw in the fridge.
  • Do not leave khoya out for more than two hours (one hour in very hot weather), as the US FDA advises for perishable food.
  • Always use a clean, dry spoon, and throw it away if it smells sour or shows mould.

Common problems and simple fixes

Khoya turned brown or smells burnt

The flame was too high or the base was not scraped. Keep the flame low, use a heavy pan and stir constantly in the last stage.

Khoya is hard and dry

It was cooked too long, or the milk was low in fat. Stop earlier next time and use whole milk.

Khoya is grainy or sour

The milk was not fresh. Grainy khoya still works for kalakand, but for smooth peda use fresh milk.

What to make with homemade khoya

Peda, burfi, gulab jamun, kalakand, gujiya filling and rich curries such as malai kofta all use khoya. If you also make paneer or ghee at home, see our guides to making paneer at home and making ghee at home from malai.

Frequently asked questions

How much khoya do I get from 1 litre of milk?

Home recipe guides report about 180–250 g of khoya from 1 litre of whole milk, depending on the milk's fat and solids and how dry you cook it. A dairy-technology course gives typical yields of about 21.6–23% from buffalo milk and 18.3–18.5% from cow milk, or roughly 1 kg of khoya from 4 kg of buffalo milk or 5 kg of cow milk.

Which milk is best for khoya: cow or buffalo?

Dairy-technology texts prefer buffalo milk for khoya because its higher fat and solids give a higher yield and a whiter, softer, smoother khoya that suits sweets. Cow milk khoya is usually harder and drier. Use whole milk; low-fat or skimmed milk gives hard, coarse khoya.

How long does it take to make khoya at home?

About 1.5 to 2.5 hours for 1 litre on a low flame in a heavy-bottomed pan, according to published home recipes. The last 10–15 minutes need constant stirring because the thick mass burns easily.

Can I make khoya without milk powder or cream?

Yes. Traditional khoya is made only from milk, by slowly evaporating its water in an open pan. Milk powder or cream are shortcuts some recipes use to save time; they are not required.

How long does homemade khoya last?

Recipe guides say about 3–7 days in a covered container in the fridge, and longer in the freezer. Keep it at 4 °C or below and do not leave it out for more than two hours, as the US FDA advises for perishable food. Throw it away if it smells sour or shows mould.
